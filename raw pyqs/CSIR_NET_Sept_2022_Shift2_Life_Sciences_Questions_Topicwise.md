### Question 21 [Question ID: 166 | Part B (Life Sciences)]

`Question Description: 121_703S2_PARTB_CSIR_SEPT22_SET1_BIL_Q21`

**Question:**
The B-form double stranded DNA was invaded by a complementary RNA sequence to form an R-loop structure. During this process,

**Options:**
1. sugar puckering on the DNA strand that pairs with RNA will remain unchanged.
2. sugar puckering on the DNA strand that pairs with RNA will change.
3. sugar puckering on the DNA strand that pairs with RNA will remain unchanged but the number of base pairs per turn in the RNA-DNA hybrid will increase.
4. sugar puckering on the DNA strand that pairs with RNA will change but the number of base pairs per turn in the RNA-DNA hybrid with remain unchanged.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Detailed Scientific Explanation</b></summary>

**Correct Answer:** Option 2 (sugar puckering on the DNA strand that pairs with RNA will change.)

**Detailed Explanation:**
In canonical double-stranded B-DNA, the 2'-deoxyribofuranose rings adopt the C2'-endo sugar pucker conformation, which spaces adjacent phosphate groups by ~7.0 A and yields a helical rise of 3.4 A per base pair with ~10.5 base pairs per helical turn. In contrast, RNA duplexes and RNA-DNA hybrids adopt an A-form-like geometry because the steric bulk of the 2'-hydroxyl (-OH) group on the ribose ring prevents the C2'-endo conformation, energetically favoring the C3'-endo conformation. When an RNA strand invades B-DNA to form an R-loop, the hybridized DNA strand is forced to transition from C2'-endo to the C3'-endo pucker to accommodate Watson-Crick base pairing with the RNA strand in the A-type hybrid duplex. Thus, the sugar puckering on the DNA strand pairing with RNA changes (Option 2).

</details>

### Question 22 [Question ID: 167 | Part B (Life Sciences)]

`Question Description: 122_703S2_PARTB_CSIR_SEPT22_SET1_BIL_Q22`

**Question:**
Which one of the following is NOT CORRECT in the context of protein structure and folding?

**Options:**
1. β-sheets are more common in the interiors of proteins than surfaces
2. β-sheets are less likely to form than α-helices in the earliest stages of protein folding
3. Proline residues can occupy the N-terminal turn of an α-helix
4. α-helices are less likely to form than β-sheets in the earliest stages of protein folding

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Detailed Scientific Explanation</b></summary>

**Correct Answer:** Option 4 (α-helices are less likely to form than β-sheets in the earliest stages of protein folding)

**Detailed Explanation:**
In the hierarchy and kinetics of protein folding, secondary structure nucleation occurs extremely rapidly. An alpha-helix is formed via local hydrogen bonds between the backbone carbonyl oxygen of residue i and the amide proton of residue i+4. Because these interacting partners are separated by only 3 intervening residues along the contiguous polypeptide chain, the entropic search space is very small and alpha-helical nucleation occurs on a sub-microsecond timescale (10^-8 to 10^-7 s). In contrast, beta-sheet formation involves non-local interactions between distal or non-contiguous beta-strands, requiring long-range chain diffusion and overcoming a substantial conformational entropic barrier, typically taking microseconds to milliseconds (10^-5 to 10^-3 s). Therefore, alpha-helices form more readily and earlier than beta-sheets, making statement 4 ('alpha-helices are less likely to form than beta-sheets in the earliest stages of protein folding') fundamentally INCORRECT and thus the correct answer.

</details>

### Question 23 [Question ID: 168 | Part B (Life Sciences)]

`Question Description: 123_703S2_PARTB_CSIR_SEPT22_SET1_BIL_Q23`

**Question:**
Which of the following represents the most oxidized form of carbon?

**Options:**
1. HCOOH
2. HCHO
3. CH3OH
4. CO2

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Detailed Scientific Explanation</b></summary>

**Correct Answer:** Option 4 (CO2)

**Detailed Explanation:**
The formal oxidation state of carbon in common biological organic compounds can be calculated by assigning oxidation numbers (H = +1, O = -2):
- In Methanol (CH3OH): C + 4(+1) + (-2) = 0 => C = -2.
- In Formaldehyde (HCHO): C + 2(+1) + (-2) = 0 => C = 0.
- In Formic acid (HCOOH): C + 2(+1) + 2(-2) = 0 => C = +2.
- In Carbon dioxide (CO2): C + 2(-2) = 0 => C = +4.
Thus, CO2 represents the fully oxidized, lowest-energy state of carbon in biological redox metabolism.

</details>

### Question 71 [Question ID: 216 | Part C (Higher Order Life Sciences)]

`Question Description: 171_703S2_PARTC_CSIR_SEPT22_SET1_BIL_Q71`

**Question:**
The F1 subunit of F0F1 ATP synthase synthesizes ATP from ADP in the mitochondrial inner membrane. Purified F1 subunit hydrolyses ATP to ADP. Which one of the following reasons explains the difference between the activities of the F1 subunit in soluble and membrane bound form?

**Options:**
1. A conformational change in the F1 subunit between the two environments
2. The lipid bilayer environment facilitates the synthesis of ATP by enhancing the rate of the dehydration reaction
3. The ATP synthesis reaction is driven by coupling to an electrochemical potential across the inner mitochondrial membrane
4. In the soluble form, the electrochemical potential drives the F1 subunit to hydrolyze ATP

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Detailed Scientific Explanation</b></summary>

**Correct Answer:** Option 3 (The ATP synthesis reaction is driven by coupling to an electrochemical potential across the inner mitochondrial membrane)

**Detailed Explanation:**
The F0F1-ATP synthase is a reversible, rotary molecular engine. At the catalytic beta-subunits of the F1 headpiece, the equilibrium constant for the condensation reaction ADP + Pi <=> ATP + H2O while bound to the catalytic site is close to 1 (delta G zero approx 0). However, high-affinity release of newly synthesized ATP requires cyclical conformational changes driven by the rotation of the central gamma-stalk. In intact mitochondria, this mechanical rotation is driven by downhill proton translocation through the membrane-embedded F0 proteolipid ring, coupled to the proton motive force (the electrochemical transmembrane potential). When F1 is detached from F0 and the membrane, there is no proton electrochemical gradient to drive rotary synthesis; consequently, the isolated soluble F1 complex functions thermodynamically in reverse as an ATP hydrolase (ATPase), hydrolyzing ATP down its spontaneous free-energy gradient (delta G zero approx -30.5 kJ/mol).

</details>

### Question 73 [Question ID: 218 | Part C (Higher Order Life Sciences)]

`Question Description: 173_703S2_PARTC_CSIR_SEPT22_SET1_BIL_Q73`

**Question:**
An enzyme has a Km of 5 x 10^-5 M and a Vmax of 100 μmoles.lit^-1.min^-1 (Km is the Michaelis constant and Vmax is the maximal velocity).
What is the velocity in the presence of 1 x 10^-4 M substrate and 2 x 10^-4 M competitive inhibitor, given that the Ki for the inhibitor is 2 x 10^-4 M?

**Options:**
1. 0.005 μmoles. lit^-1. min^-1
2. 50 μmoles. lit^-1. min^-1
3. 5 μmoles. lit^-1. min^-1
4. 500 μmoles. lit^-1. min^-1

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Detailed Scientific Explanation</b></summary>

**Correct Answer:** Option 2 (50 μmoles. lit^-1. min^-1)

**Detailed Explanation:**
In competitive inhibition, the inhibitor competes directly with the substrate for the free active site. The apparent Michaelis constant (Km_app) is increased by the factor alpha = 1 + [I]/Ki, while Vmax remains unchanged.
Given:
- Km = 5 x 10^-5 M
- Vmax = 100 umol/L/min
- [I] = 2 x 10^-4 M
- Ki = 2 x 10^-4 M
- [S] = 1 x 10^-4 M

Step 1: Calculate alpha:
alpha = 1 + [I]/Ki = 1 + (2 x 10^-4) / (2 x 10^-4) = 1 + 1 = 2.

Step 2: Calculate Km_app:
Km_app = alpha * Km = 2 * (5 x 10^-5 M) = 1 x 10^-4 M.

Step 3: Calculate reaction velocity v:
Notice that the substrate concentration [S] = 1 x 10^-4 M = Km_app. By definition of the Michaelis constant, when [S] = Km_app:
v = Vmax * [S] / (Km_app + [S]) = Vmax / 2 = 100 / 2 = 50 umol.lit^-1.min^-1.

</details>

### Question 74 [Question ID: 219 | Part C (Higher Order Life Sciences)]

`Question Description: 174_703S2_PARTC_CSIR_SEPT22_SET1_BIL_Q74`

**Question:**
Pyruvate kinase, the enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of PEP to pyruvate transfers the Pi from PEP to ADP to generate ATP. The standard free energies of the half-reactions are given below.

PEP + H2O <==> Pyruvate (enol form) + HPO4^2-   (ΔG°' = -16 kJ·mol^-1)
ADP + Pi <==> ATP   (ΔG°' = +30.5 kJ·mol^-1)

How is the free energy for generation of ATP from ADP derived in the reaction catalyzed by pyruvate kinase?

**Options:**
1. through coupling with keto-enol tautomerism where the enol form of pyruvate is converted to the keto form
2. through condensation of Pi with ADP
3. through linking to proton motive force
4. through coupling with hydrolysis of PPi

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Detailed Scientific Explanation</b></summary>

**Correct Answer:** Option 1 (through coupling with keto-enol tautomerism where the enol form of pyruvate is converted to the keto form)

**Detailed Explanation:**
The transfer of a phosphate group from phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to ADP is catalyzed by pyruvate kinase in step 10 of glycolysis. The overall reaction has a highly exergonic standard free-energy change (delta G zero prime = -31.4 kJ/mol), making it essentially irreversible.
The reaction occurs in two concerted chemical stages:
1. Hydrolysis of the ester phosphate bond: Cleavage of the phosphate from PEP generates an enol-pyruvate intermediate with a modest standard free energy of delta G zero prime approx -16 kJ/mol.
2. Keto-enol tautomerization: The enol form of pyruvate rapidly and spontaneously tautomerizes to the substantially more stable keto form. This non-enzymatic tautomerization has an enormous negative standard free-energy change (delta G zero prime approx -46 kJ/mol).
Together, the overall free energy of PEP hydrolysis is -16 + (-46) = -61.9 kJ/mol. This large driving force easily overcomes the +30.5 kJ/mol required to synthesize ATP from ADP and Pi. Thus, the extra free energy is derived through coupling with keto-enol tautomerism where the enol form of pyruvate is converted to the keto form (Option 1).

</details>

### Question 75 [Question ID: 220 | Part C (Higher Order Life Sciences)]

`Question Description: 175_703S2_PARTC_CSIR_SEPT22_SET1_BIL_Q75`

**Question:**
Which of the four molecules shown below are optically active?

A. trans-3,6-dimethylpiperazine-2,5-dione (diketopiperazine ring with trans-methyl groups: C(CH3)(H)-NH-CO-C(H)(CH3)-NH-CO)
B. 1-R-trans-2,3-dimethylcyclopropane (cyclopropane ring with substituent R and H at C1, and trans methyl groups at C2 and C3)
C. meso-Cystine: HOOC-CH(NH2)-CH2-S-S-CH2-CH(NH2)-COOH with (R, S) configuration
D. (R,R)-Selenocystine: HOOC-CH(NH2)-CH2-Se-Se-CH2-CH(NH2)-COOH with identical configurations at both chiral centers

**Options:**
1. A, B, C and D
2. B and D only
3. A and C only
4. B only

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Detailed Scientific Explanation</b></summary>

**Correct Answer:** Option 2 (B and D only)

**Detailed Explanation:**
Optical activity requires molecular chirality, meaning the molecule must not possess any improper axis of rotation (Sn), which specifically includes an internal plane of symmetry (sigma = S1) or a center of inversion (i = S2):
- Molecule A (trans-diketopiperazine): Features a center of inversion (i) at the center of the six-membered heterocyclic ring relating the two trans-methyl and opposite carbonyl/NH groups. Because it has a center of inversion, it is centrosymmetric, achiral, and optically inactive.
- Molecule B (trans-2,3-dimethylcyclopropane with a substituent R at C1): With one methyl group pointing up and the other pointing down, there is no mirror plane bisecting C1 and the C2-C3 bond. The molecule is non-superimposable on its mirror image (chiral) and therefore optically active.
- Molecule C (meso-cystine): The two chiral carbons have opposite absolute configurations (2R, 2'S). An internal mirror plane bisects the disulfide bond, making this a classic meso compound that is achiral and optically inactive.
- Molecule D (chiral selenocystine): Both chiral centers possess the same absolute configuration (either R,R or S,S). The molecule has C2 rotational symmetry but lacks any mirror plane or inversion center; therefore, it is chiral and optically active.
Hence, B and D only are optically active (Option 2).

</details>
